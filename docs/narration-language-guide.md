# Multilingual spoken-script guide

Use this guide whenever a user requests narration in another language, a regional dialect, local slang, or natural multilingual speech. The narration follows the requested language and style. Keep other project writing in English unless the user explicitly asks to localize it.

## Write as people speak

- Compose directly in the requested language and conversational style. Never draft in English and translate sentence by sentence.
- Prefer current, everyday speech over literary, formal, textbook, or outdated phrasing unless the user requests it.
- Use short and medium-length sentences that flow aloud. Explain complex ideas in simple connected thoughts, with natural questions, pauses, reactions, and transitions.
- Start from a relatable situation when it fits. Introduce the topic naturally, explain it through a useful example, and finish with a natural conclusion or call to action when appropriate.
- Match the speaker, audience, relationship, politeness, emotion, context, and requested tone. Let delivery be calm or energetic, restrained or emphatic, with pauses, humor, or slang when they fit naturally.

## Respect regional speech

- Treat a requested dialect, city, region, accent, or slang as a primary requirement. Let it shape vocabulary, grammar, expressions, and speaking rhythm throughout the narration, not just a few inserted words.
- Apply regional speech consistently and naturally. Do not assume everyone from a region speaks alike or rely on stereotypes and canned catchphrases. Use colloquial expressions, slang, and stylized delivery when they fit the audience and requested tone; check unfamiliar local expressions rather than inventing them.

## Keep code-switching natural

- Preserve familiar borrowed words, technical terms, and workplace vocabulary where speakers naturally use them.
- Keep the requested language's natural grammar and sentence structure. Avoid forcing pure-language equivalents or switching whole sentences unnecessarily.
- Prefer everyday multilingual speech over formal or literary written-language equivalents when that matches how speakers naturally talk. Preserve familiar borrowed terms in the language mix; do not force formal equivalents just because they are available.
- Let the language mix follow the speaker, audience, region, and situation. Do not impose a limit on borrowed words, English terms, or longer code-switched passages when they fit the requested style.

## Keep examples accurate

- Use specific, believable situations to explain a product or idea. Prefer practical examples over generic praise.
- Preserve the user's facts, brand, intent, and constraints. Never invent product features, automation, results, guarantees, testimonials, or statistics.
- Clearly distinguish suggestions and support from actions the service performs. State user review or approval steps when they matter.

## Prepare for TTS

- Prefer the native script for the requested language. Keep code-switched terms in their natural form when that helps pronunciation and fluency.
- Avoid excessive phonetic spelling, repeated vowels, hyphenation, and unnecessary punctuation. Use phonetic spelling only when the voice needs it.
- Preserve natural code-switching without limiting English terms or phrase length to manage accent. Direct TTS to keep the same requested voice, accent, and cadence across Telugu and English, including longer English passages; do not switch to an English accent when the language changes. Use pronunciation guidance only for specific names or terms that need it, and do not transliterate every borrowed word.
- Add brief pronunciation guidance for unusual brand names, abbreviations, or technical terms when needed, and apply the preferred pronunciation consistently.
- Keep voice direction short and precise. Include only relevant details such as language or dialect, speaker tone, pace, code-switching, pronunciation, and important emphasis.
- Keep voice directions separate from the spoken script. Never put performance instructions inside the TTS narration block.

## Naturalness check

Before handoff, silently check that a native speaker could say the script naturally, the requested dialect is present without exaggeration, code-switching feels spontaneous, sentences flow aloud, examples are useful, claims are supported, and names are pronounceable. Rewrite any part that sounds translated, robotic, forced, or overly polished. Do not add explanations, translations, or linguistic analysis unless asked.

For video projects, keep the narration in the requested language while following the separate English defaults for captions, cover text, visual beats, and other project writing unless the user explicitly requests those in another language.
